The opportunity

Turn historical data into a clearer next decision.

Use your historical data to explore likely outcomes and support planning. We establish a baseline, build and compare suitable models, and test them against data they have not seen. Forecasts are presented with assumptions and limitations so your team can use them alongside business judgement.

Where it can fit

Useful starting points

  • Product demand and stock planning
  • Customer churn prioritisation
  • Capacity and workload forecasting

In practice

Use past results to make a planning estimate

A forecast estimates what may happen next based on past records. A stock planner might compare last year's demand with more recent patterns and use the estimate as one input when deciding what to order.

Define the question and time period

We agree whether the team needs a weekly, monthly, or other estimate and which products or locations it should cover.

Check against known periods

We test the method by asking what it would have predicted in the past and comparing that with what actually happened.

Fit the result into a decision

We can show the estimate with relevant context, such as unusual periods or likely variation, so staff can review before acting.

What to share with us

Share historical records, dates of unusual events, and the planning decision the forecast should support.

Before we start

Questions worth answering.

How much historical data do we need?

It depends on the prediction horizon, seasonality, granularity and business changes. We review the available history before suggesting an approach.

Are forecasts guaranteed?

No. Forecasts are estimates and can be affected by events outside the data. We discuss uncertainty, assumptions and appropriate review points.

Can forecasting fit our current dashboard?

Yes, where the dashboard or its data layer supports integration. We agree the refresh process and how users should interpret the outputs.

Can we forecast a new product with little history?

Possibly through related products, business attributes or broader planning assumptions, but uncertainty is often greater. The available evidence determines what is reasonable to model.

Why compare with a simple baseline?

A baseline shows whether a more complex model adds useful value. It also provides a fallback and helps make model performance easier to interpret.
